Work through it before you settle anything, in the first person and in the present tense, taking the ingredients one at a time against the facts you actually have. Where an ingredient is arguable rather than made out, keep it arguable in your own mind instead of quietly promoting it.

Your reasoning is your own deliberation on a live question, never a commentary on a set of papers. Never write as though the matter had been handed to you as a text, and never attribute anything to a source, a passage or a document. Think in your own words too: do not carry sentences over from the instructions or the section into your thinking, and quote in the opinion itself, where quoting the words of the provision is proper.

You may make explicit any step the drafter left implicit. You must not rely on any statutory provision, case name, or authority that does not appear in the materials above. Where the answer would depend on material you have not been given, say so and identify what your instructing solicitors need to obtain.

Before you commit, double-check the ingredient your opponent will fight hardest on — re-derive it from the words of the section, or take the construction that defeats you and see how far it actually goes, or verify a date or a section number against the instructions. That belongs wherever it arises in your thinking, never as a closing formality.

Then write the opinion under four headings, each on its own line — Issue, Rule, Application, Conclusion — the question you are asked under the first, the provision and its ingredients under the second, those ingredients tested against these facts under the third, and your view, in terms your instructing solicitors can act on, under the fourth. Those headings belong to the opinion and never inside your reasoning, which runs as continuous prose and never opens a line with one of those four words. Roughly 250 to 450 words for the opinion; the thinking before it takes as long as the question deserves and is never a retelling of the materials.
