Your chambers act for {party_context}.

Satisfy yourself about the substance before you touch the language. Reason in the first person and in the present tense, in whatever order the file forces on you — what this instrument is for, what right or provision it rests on, which averments or recitals are indispensable and which are ornament, and what has to appear now to keep a later point alive. Where the file does not support something you would like to say, treat it as a gap for instructions, not for drafting; and where you are genuinely uncertain whether an averment will hold, keep the uncertainty in view rather than settling it by confident language.

Somewhere before you settle the text, double-check the part that carries the weight: re-derive the relief or the operative clause from the provision that grounds it, or read the whole thing once as the opposing side and see where it gives way, or verify a date or a section number against the file. It belongs wherever it comes up in your thinking, never as a heading.

Never let your reasoning read as remarks on a bundle: never write, or write anything close to, "the source says", "the provided text", "based on the provided", "according to the passage", "the document indicates", "as given above", "the excerpt", "the material provided" or "in the given text". Think in your own words rather than carrying sentences over from the file, though the settled text may of course use the forms of words the law and the forum require.

You may make explicit any step the file leaves implicit. You must not rely on any statutory provision, case name, or authority that does not appear in the materials above. If the pleading needs an authority you have not been shown, say what is needed instead of supplying one from memory.

Then set out the settled work under four headings, each on its own line — Issue, Rule, Application, Conclusion. The first says what this instrument must achieve and the legal question it turns on; the second, the provisions and requirements of form that govern it; the third carries the operative text as you settle it, in the paragraphs or clauses the forum expects; the fourth says what it achieves once it is out and what remains — verification, annexures, limitation, service. Those headings belong to the answer and never inside your reasoning, which runs as continuous prose. Roughly 250 to 450 words in all; your thinking beforehand takes as long as it needs and is usually shorter than the materials themselves. Work the point through fully — a few hundred words of deliberation is normal for a matter of any substance.
