Before you settle the advice, think the position through properly and in your own voice — first person, present tense, the way you would actually turn a problem over before you pick up the pen. Where you are genuinely unsure, be unsure on the page instead of writing round it.

When the thinking is done, write the advice. Set it out under four headings, each on its own line — Issue, Rule, Application, Conclusion — identifying under the first what precisely has to be decided for your client, under the second the law that governs it, under the third that law applied to these facts and to this client's position in them, and under the fourth what follows, said plainly enough for a lay client to act on. Those headings belong to the advice and never inside your reasoning, which runs as continuous prose and never opens a line with one of those four words. Aim at roughly 250 to 450 words for the advice; the reasoning runs as long as it needs to and is never a retelling of them. Work the point through fully — 450 to 700 words of deliberation is normal for a matter of any substance.

Nothing in your thinking may refer to this brief as a text put in front of you. Never write as though the matter had been handed to you as a text, and never attribute anything to a source, a passage or a document. This is your client's matter; you are reasoning about the case, not about a description of it.

You may make explicit any step the court left implicit. You must not rely on any statutory provision, case name, or authority that does not appear in the materials above. If the point turns on something the papers do not tell you, name the gap and advise around it rather than filling it with an authority you cannot produce.

Somewhere before you conclude, double-check yourself: take the answer the other side will give and test it seriously, or re-derive a step you took quickly, or confirm a date or a section number against the papers. Let that come where it belongs in the flow of your thinking, not as a set-piece at the end. And think in your own words — do not lift sentences out of the papers into your reasoning; save quotation for the advice itself, where quoting a provision or a holding is proper. Say the doubt in the words you would actually use — "let me check", "actually,", "to confirm", "am I sure", "let me reconsider".
